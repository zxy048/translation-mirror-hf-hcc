Dear Editor,

We submit for consideration at *Nature Cardiovascular Research* our manuscript entitled **"Distinct networks drive mirror perturbation of translation-related transcription in heart failure and hepatocellular carcinoma."**

**Importance and novelty.** Heart failure and cancer are conventionally studied in separate disciplinary silos, yet both exhibit profound dysregulation of translation — the most energetically expensive step of gene expression — in opposite directions (suppression in the failing heart, activation in the proliferating tumor). Whether these opposite perturbations reflect a conserved transcriptional program operating in two disease contexts, or disease-specific programs converging on shared functional themes, has remained unknown. This question cannot be answered within single-disease frameworks.

Our study provides the first systematic cross-disease comparative analysis of translation-related transcriptional programs in heart failure and hepatocellular carcinoma. Using identical computational pipelines across multiple independent cohorts, we report three key findings: (1) translation-related modules exhibit disease-specific co-expression network organization with limited gene-level overlap, rather than module conservation; (2) despite this network divergence, 24 of 33 translation-related pathways show convergent mirror perturbation at the pathway level — the strongest quantitative signal in the study; and (3) this mirror pattern is absent in same-organ disease controls, supporting disease-context dependence rather than organ-specific baseline confounding. We introduce the framework of **disease-context-dependent remodeling** — shared functional themes emerging through distinct network configurations with opposite perturbation directions — and identify ATF4/ISR (stress-responsive) and MYC (proliferative) as complementary regulatory dimensions associated with translation-related remodeling.

**Fit for *Nature Cardiovascular Research*.** This work bridges cardiovascular biology and cancer biology through the unifying lens of translational regulation — a molecular process of fundamental importance to both fields. The cross-disease, multi-resolution analytical framework should be of interest to the diverse readership of *Nature Cardiovascular Research*, including cardiovascular biologists, cancer biologists, and translational researchers. The conceptual model we propose — disease-context-dependent remodeling — offers a framework for future cross-disease comparative studies that may stimulate discussion beyond the cardiovascular community.

A B S T R A C T
SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is highly aggressive with multiple oncogene mutations, and 
has low sensitivity to chemotherapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy. By integrating multi-omics analysis 
and machine learning analysis on 20 lung adenocarcinoma datasets, we characterized SMARCA4 mutation in 
clinical prognosis, metabolic level, immune infiltration and pathway enrichment. The key role of SMARCA4 in 
LUAD development was confirmed by several in vitro and in vivo experiments. SMARCA4 is highly expressed in 
LUAD tissues compared with adjacent tissues, and SMARCA4 mutation is a poor prognostic factor. Although 
SMARCA4-deficient LUAD patients are not sensitive to common chemotherapeutic drugs, immunotherapy is an 
alternative treatment for lung adenocarcinoma. SMARCA4 deletion is associated with a high rate of oncogene co- 
mutation. Co-mutation of KRAS was found to have a significant impact on the response to multiple types of 
chemotherapy. Unique metabolic profiles, abnormal cell cycle and hyperactivation of oxidative phosphorylation 
were hallmarks of SMARCA4 mutant LUAD. We screened four chemotherapeutic agents targeting MTOR 
pathway, oxidative stress, DNA replication or cell cycle, including rapamycin and ribociclib, as potential 
chemotherapeutic agents for SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma. In vitro assays showed that SMARCA4 
knockdown significantly inhibited DNA replication, cell cycle and cell proliferation in lung adenocarcinoma 
cells, and significantly promoted oxidative stress and apoptosis in lung adenocarcinoma cells. Our comprehen­
sive findings advance our understanding of SMARCA4 mutant LUAD gene mutation and immune microenvi­
ronment, providing insights into potential therapeutic approaches and research directions for SMARCA4- 
deficient LUAD.
1. Introduction
SWI/SNF (SWItch/Sucrose NonFermentable) chromatin remodeling 
complex is a member of the five subfamilies of ATP-dependent chro­
mosome remodeling complexes. SWI/SNF complex regulates gene ac­
tivity or expression by altering DNA-nucleosome topology, thereby 
changing chromatin from a dense to an open state during gene tran­
scription [1,2]. SWI/SNF complexes are involved in a variety of key 
cellular biological processes, including chromatin repair, cell prolifera­
tion and cell differentiation [3]. There are 10 to 15 subunits in the SWI/ 
SNF complex, and different combinations of subunits produce various 
complexes, with some core subunits required for complex assembly, 
especially SMARCB1, SMARCA2, SMARCA4, ARID1A, and ARID1B [4]. 
As an ATP-dependent catalytic subunit of SWI/SNF chromosome 
remodeling complex, SMARCA4 participates in the regulation of chro­
matin structure and gene expression by providing energy [5]. Studies

have shown that SMARCA4 is involved in several key biological pro­
cesses in tumor cells. SMARCA4 ensures the orderly progression of 
mitosis by recruiting topoisomerases and regulates G1/S phase DNA 
transcription [6,7]. Meanwhile, SMARCA4 is involved in regulating the 
expression of tumorigenic transmembrane glycoprotein CD44, thus 
participating in the regulation of cell adhesion and metastasis [8].
Mutations in the SWI/SNF complex are widely distributed in various 
human tumors, and these mutations are most commonly found in the 
SMARCA4 subunit [9]. Mutant inactivation of SMARCA4 alters chro­
matin accessibility and transcriptional regulation of genes, resulting in 
an invasive phenotype [10]. Studies have shown that heterozygous mice 
with lung SMARCA4 protein conditional knockout have significantly 
increased tumor volume [11]. Results from a public database show that 
SMARCA4 mutated lung adenocarcinoma exhibit the same highly 
dedifferentiated state as the relevant mouse models [12]. SMARCA4- 
dNSCLC (SMARCA4-deficient non-small lung cancer) accounts for at 
least 10 % of all cases of NSCLC (non-small lung cancer) [13], and is 
associated with the aggressiveness and poor prognosis of NSCLC [14]. 
Recent studies have shown that NSCLC patients with SMARCA4 muta­
tions, especially homozygous deletions and truncating mutations, are 
more likely to have drug resistance, early recurrence, and poor clinical 
outcomes than patients with wild-type SMARCA4 [10,15].
Regarding chemotherapy, SMARCA4 was shown to promote efficient 
repair of cisplatin-induced DNA damage and recruitment of the DNA 
repair factor ERCC1 after damage recognition [16]. In addition, 
SMARCA4-dNSCLC was more sensitive to platinum-based chemo­
therapy, and cisplatin-vincristine adjuvant therapy significantly 
improved the disease-specific survival of SMARCA4-low stage IB/II 
NSCLC patients [17]. However, a small retrospective study showed that 
platinum-based doublet chemotherapy or chemotherapy combined with 
immunotherapy did not significantly improve the prognosis of 
SMARCA4-dNSCLC patients [18]. In terms of immunotherapy, 
compared with SMARCA4 unmutated patients, most patients with 
SMARCA4 mutation have low or negative expression of PD-L1 (pro­
grammed cell death ligand 1) and high TMB (Tumor mutation burden) 
[14,19]. Although high TMB is often associated with a better immuno­
therapy response rate in lung adenocarcinoma, the theoretical mecha­
nism for this phenomenon is that TMB in lung adenocarcinoma is 
positively correlated with CD8+ T cell infiltration [20], and SMARCA4 
mutation has been shown to reduce CD8+ T cell infiltration [21]. In 
addition, SMARCA4 mutation patients may be in an immunosuppressive 
microenvironment [22,23], suggesting that SMARCA4-deficient lung 
adenocarcinoma patients are less likely to benefit from immunotherapy.
Diversified machine learning (ML) algorithm is a data analysis 
method that helps to find therapeutic targets for lung cancer by learning 
from data to predict results [24]. The clinical applications of ML range 
from diagnosis to prediction and have been applied in various clinical 
fields [25–28]. Since there is currently no effective treatment for 
SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma, we used multi-omics inte­
grative analysis and machine learning methods to simulate the in vivo 
environment to explore the causes and potential mechanisms of drug 
resistance and poor prognosis in SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarci­
noma. We analyzed the differences in the effects of SMARCA4 deletion 
mutations and SMARCA4 low expression on the prognosis, immuno­
therapy, chemotherapy, and cell metabolism of lung adenocarcinoma, 
and proposed potential therapeutic modalities. This study explored 
common SMARCA4 co-mutated genes and analyzed the effect of co- 
mutated genes on susceptibility to chemotherapy in patients with 
SMARCA4 mutation deletion. We used multi-dataset enrichment anal­
ysis and in vivo/vitro assays to analyze and verify the differences in in 
vivo and in vitro function of SMARCA4 and the metabolic characteristics 
of SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma. This study provides po­
tential therapeutic targets and research direction for the clinical treat­
ment of SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma.

2.1. Bioinformatics and ethics
2.1.1. Clinical samples
This study received approval from the Biomedical Research Ethics 
Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital (SWYX: NO. 2024–711) and 
was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All pa­
tients were granted written informed consent for the scientific use of

2.1.2. Data collection and preprocessing
The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data encompassed RNA expression 
matrix, and associated clinical data for 33 types of carcinomas, including 
LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma). These data were downloaded from the 
UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/). RNA-seq data under­
went log2 transformation for normalization.
Data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi. 
nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database for SMARCA4 differential expression 
analysis were screened according to the following criteria: (1) lung 
adenocarcinoma; (2) human gene expression profiles; (3) published 
between 2007 and 2022; (4) case-control groups; (5) sample size greater 
than 10; (6) publicly available. Finally, eight GEO datasets were 
included in the analysis, namely GES31210, GSE31547, GSE40791, 
GSE32863, GSE19188, GSE75037, GSE30219, and GSE10072.
Data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi. 
nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database for SMARCA4 prognosis analysis were 
screened according to the following criteria: (1) lung adenocarcinoma; 
(2) human gene expression profiles; (3) published between 2007 and 
2022; (4) sample size greater than 50; (5) publicly available; (6) prog­
nostic information included. Finally, five GEO datasets were included in 
the analysis, namely GSE30219, GSE37745, GSE50081, GSE68465, 
GSE72094.
There is also an immunotherapy clinical cohort (IMvigor210) 
available at http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies.
Immunohistochemical images of lung adenocarcinoma and lung 
normal tissues downloaded from THE HUMAN PROTEIN ATLAS 
(https://www.proteinatlas.org/).
2.1.3. Immune infiltration analyses
Cibersort algorithm analysis was used to obtain a matrix of different 
immune cell percentages in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. SMARCA4-related 
immune cells were grouped from two perspectives (top 10 % of 
SMARCA4 high expression samples and bottom 10 % of SMARCA4 low 
expression samples, SMARCA4 mutant and non-mutant). Differential 
analysis was performed using “limma” package to identify SMARCA4- 
related immune cells.
The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE, http://tide. 
dfci.harvard.edu/) Website and TIDE scores were used to predict the 
effect of SMARCA4 on immunotherapy response in TCGA-LUAD dataset.
2.1.4. Chemotherapy response prediction
To investigate the role of SMARCA4 in chemotherapy, R package 
“oncoPredict” was utilized to predict the drug sensitivity concerning 
common chemotherapeutic drugs in TCGA-LUAD cohort.

2.1.6. Enrichment analysis
To explore the SMARCA4-related functions and pathways, we 
analyzed SMARCA4 expression levels in TCGA-LUAD, GSE236587, and

from two perspectives (the high 10 % SMARCA4 expression of samples

enrichment analysis (GSEA), KEGG and GO analysis were used to reveal

“clusterprofiler”. In addition, “clusterprofiler” was also used for ssGSEA 
analysis to score immune cells, metabolism, cell behavior, and related 
pathways.
2.1.7. Pan-cancer analysis
The R package “limma” compared SMARCA4 expressions across 33 
pan-cancers using combined GTEx and TCGA data. Univariate Cox 
regression and Kaplan—Meier curves were performed in pan-cancer 
analysis.
2.2. Machine learning
Models were constructed using XGBoost, Support Vector Machine 
(SVM), or Random Forest (RF) algorithms. The 45 patients with 
SMARCA4 mutations in the TCGA-LUAD dataset were divided into 
training set and validation set by K-fold cross validation. The best K 
value was determined by evaluating the prediction accuracy of K-fold 
cross validation, which was 5, 6 and 5 for the three models XGBoost, 
SVM and RF, respectively. According to logFC >1 and p < 0.05, the 
differentially expressed genes between SMARCA4 mutated and non- 
mutated samples in TCGA-LUAD dataset were screened. The expres­
sion values of 100 differentially expressed genes were used as model 
features, and TIDE predicted whether patients responded to immuno­
therapy was used as model labels. The model performance was evalu­
ated using Area under Curve (AUC), confusion matrix and accuracy 
evaluation. We optimized several parameters of the XGBoost algorithm 
using the Naive Bayes algorithm. The Naive Bayes algorithm is a clas­
sification method based on Bayes' theorem and the assumption of feature 
conditional independence. For a given training set, firstly, the joint 
probability distribution of input/output is learned based on the 
assumption of conditional independence of features. Subsequently, for 
the given input data, Bayes' theorem is used to derive the output result 
with the maximum posterior probability. SVM algorithm adopts the 
hyperparameter optimization mode of grid search combined with cross 
validation and uses weighted F1 score as the optimization objective. RF 
algorithm adopts the hyperparameter optimization mode of random 
search combined with cross validation and uses weighted F1 score as the 
optimization objective. All model hyperparameter tuning was carried 
out exclusively on the training data. See the Supplementary Material for 
details of the code. Finally, the XGBoost algorithm was used to establish 
the model [30]. The visual analysis of the model was performed using 
Shapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) [31]. Based on cooperative game 
theory, an additive interpretation model was constructed using SHAP, 
and all features were regarded as “contributors”.
2.3. In vivo and in vitro experiments
2.3.1. Cell culture
The lung adenocarcinoma cell lines PC-9 and NCI-H1975 were pro­
cured from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). 
Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 (VivaCell). All cell lines were 
supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin- 
streptomycin (P/S) and were incubated at 37 ◦C with 5 % CO2. Impor­
tantly, the cell lines were confirmed to be free from mycoplasma 
contamination.
2.3.2. Mouse xenograft model
Four-week-old female nude mice (BALB/C nude) were obtained from 
Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, 
China). The animal room temperature was 22 ± 2 ◦C and the relative 
humidity was 50 % ± 15 %. Experiments were performed under a 12-h 
dark/light cycle. For mouse experiments, each mouse received a dorsal 
injection of 3 × 106 PC-9 cells in 200 μL mixed solution (PBS: Matrigel 
(BD356234) = 1: 1). Tumor sizes were measured every 3 days with a 
linear caliper. Tumor volume (V) was calculated using the following 
formula: (width)2 × height / 2. Euthanasia was performed 3 weeks after 
tumor transplantation. The experimental design conformed to the 
ethical requirements of animal protection and laboratory animal wel­
fare. The study was approved by the ethics committee (No. 
SWYX2024–711).
2.3.3. Lentivirus transfection
The RNAi lentiviral vector for SMARCA4 was hU6-MCS-CBh-gcGFP- 
IRES-puromycin, which was provided by Genechem, Shanghai, China. 
Following vector construction, the PC-9 and NCI-H1975 cell lines were 
transfected with virus concentration at MOI of 10 and 50 for 12 h, 
respectively. After this transfection period, the cells were switched back 
to their standard culture medium and incubated for an additional 48 h. 
Subsequently, a 1-week screening process was initiated using puromycin 
(2 μg/mL, provided by Beyotime Biotech). The stable transfected cell 
lines were used for cell viability assay and animal experiments.

2.3.7. Cell proliferation assay
Cell proliferation was measured by sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay 
and cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1500 cells/well. 
After 24 h of culture, when the cells were fully adherent, the initial cell 
adherence was recorded at zero hour, and subsequent samples were 
collected every 24 h. Each well was fixed with 80 μL trichloroacetic acid 
(TCA) for at least 6 h at 4 ◦C. After fixation, the plates were rinsed five 
times with tap water and dried overnight at room temperature. Subse­
quently, each well was stained with 0.057 % (wt/vol) SRB solution (100 
μL) for 30 min, followed by washing at least five times with 1 % acetic 
acid and drying overnight at room temperature. Each well was treated 
with 150 μL of 10 mM Tris alkaline solution and shaken at 2000 rpm for 
20 s on an IKA MS 3 digital device (Thermo Fisher, USA). The absor­
bance of the solution at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader 
(Bio-Rad, USA).
2.3.8. Cell viability assay
The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of cells was 
detected by sulforhodamine B (SRB) method. Briefly, cells were spread 
in 96-well dishes at a density of 5000 cells per well. After 24 h, when all 
cells were adherent, cisplatin (HY-17394, MCE), paclitaxel (HY-B0015, 
MCE), gemcitabine (IG0070, Solarbio), etoposide (HY-13629, MCE), 
doxorubicin (SC0159, Beyotime), ribociclib (SC6668, Beyotime), rapa­
mycin (HY-10219, MCE), IACS-010759 (HY-112037, MCE) or enaside­
nib (SD7252, Beyotime) were added for 24 h or 48 h, respectively. Cells 
were fixed with 60 μL of 10 % cold trichloroacetic acid (TCA) in each 
well and incubated for at least 6 h at 4 ◦C. They were washed five times 
with purified water and allowed to dry overnight at room temperature. 
100 μL 0.057 % (wt/vol) SRB solution was added to each well for 20 min 
for staining. Plates were washed with 1 % acetic acid and stored over­
night at room temperature. Plates were incubated with 150 μL of 10 mM 
Tris and shaken. The absorbance value at 540 nm was measured using a 
microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA), and the results were analyzed 
using GraphPad Prism8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, USA).
2.3.9. Colony formation assay
The colony formation assay was performed by culturing cells in 6- 
well plates at a density of 500 cells/well in a 5 % CO2 incubator at 
37 ◦C for 2 weeks. Afterward, the cells in each well were fixed with 
paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 500 μL of 0.1 % crystal 
violet for at least 20 min. Quantification was conducted using ImageJ 
software.
2.3.10. EdU assay
Cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 40 % density. After 24 h of 
culture, when cells were fully adherent, the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell pro­
liferation kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime biotech, C0071L) was used 
for analysis according to the manufacturer's instructions. Images were 
taken with a ZEISS fluorescence inverted microscope, processed with 
ZEN Blue Lite, and cell counting was performed using ImageJ software.
2.3.11. Annexin V-FITC/PI cell apoptosis detection
Cells were plated at 50 % density in 24-well plates. After 24 h of 
culture, when the cells were completely attached to the wall, the cells 
were collected after enzymatic digestion and centrifugation. Cells were 
washed with precooled PBS, stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI kit (YEA­
SEN, 40302), and analyzed with BD LSRFortessa and FlowJo v10.
2.3.12. Glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and 
malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement
GSH assay kit (A006–1-1), SOD assay kit (A001–3-2) and MDA assay 
kit (A003–1-2) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering 
Institute. The cells were plated in a 10 cm dish at 70 % density. After 24 
h of culture, when the cells had adhered completely, all experiments 
were performed in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.
2.3.13. Cell cycle analysis
Cells at 50 % density were seeded in 6-well plates. After 24 h of 
culture, the cells were completely adherent. Cells were washed with PBS 
and isolated by enzymatic digestion. The enzymatic separation was 
terminated with medium containing FBS. The cells were centrifuged. 
The residue was re-suspended in 70 % pre-cooled ethanol solution and 
fixed at −20 ◦C for at least 4 h. The cells were centrifuged and washed 
twice with PBS. Cells were re-suspended in 0.5 mL PI/RNase staining 
buffer (550825, BD, Shanghai, China) and incubated for 15 min at room 
temperature in the dark. Cell cycles were determined using a Cytoflex 
cell analyzer and analyzed using FlowJo v10.
2.3.14. Determination of ROS levels
Cells were incubated with 10 mM 2′, 7′ - dichlorofluorescein diac­
etate (DCFH-DA) (S0033S, Beyotime) for 20 min and then washed three 
times with PBS. The fluorescence intensity was then measured using a 
flow cytometer (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA).
2.4. Statistical analysis
All statistical data analyses were conducted using R software 
(version 4.2.1). Parts of the statistical analyses were performed by

was considered to be statistically significant. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01 and

3. Results
3.1. SMARCA4 is highly expressed in lung adenocarcinoma
To evaluate SMARCA4 expression in lung adenocarcinoma, we 
analyzed mRNA levels in tumor and normal tissues using eight GEO lung 
adenocarcinoma datasets and TCGA lung adenocarcinoma dataset 
(TCGA-LUAD). SMARCA4 expression was significantly higher in tumor 
tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1A and B). This finding 
was further validated by gel electrophoresis, which demonstrated 
elevated SMARCA4 mRNA levels in 16 paired samples of lung adeno­
carcinoma and adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 1C and D). Additionally, 
immunohistochemistry data from The Human Protein Atlas confirmed 
significantly increased SMARCA4 protein expression in lung adenocar­
cinoma tissues relative to normal lung tissues (Fig. 1E-G).

significant impact on the prognosis of patients with lung adenocarcinoma

database. The prognostic analysis results from all four datasets sug­
gested that SMARCA4 mutation patients had a poor prognosis, with p =

3.3. SMARCA4 mutations are often accompanied by high mutations in 
tumor-associated genes
Previous studies have demonstrated that patients with SMARCA4 
mutation-deletion NSCLC have higher mutation rates of KRAS, TP53, 
STK11, and other tumor-related genes. We conducted SMARCA4 co- 
mutation analysis using the respective largest lung adenocarcinoma 
dataset from five databases with no duplicates in the cBioPortal data­
base. The results suggested that the genes with a high mutation rate in 
the SMARCA4 mutant group were TP53, KEAP1, KRAS, STK11, and TTN 
(Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Further, specificity analysis re­
sults indicated that the genes that specifically mutated in the SMARCA4- 
mutant group mainly included ACIN1, OTUD6A, PHF14, KEAP1, and 
STK11 (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3). Meanwhile, EGFR muta­
tions are mutually exclusive with SMARCA4 mutations. The results 
suggested that SMARCA4 mutation was associated with a variety of 
tumor-related gene mutations, which may be a potential factor for the 
resistance of SMARCA4-deletion mutant lung adenocarcinoma to 
chemotherapy and immunotherapy.
3.4. Immunotherapy is one of the treatment options for SMARCA4 
mutation patients
The prognostic analysis of the four lung adenocarcinoma mutation 
datasets treated with immunotherapy suggested that overall survival 
(OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were not statistically different 
between SMARCA4-mutated and SMARCA4-unmutated lung adenocar­
cinoma patients treated with immunotherapy (Fig. 3A, p > 0.05). 
SMARCA4-mutant patients had a worse prognosis in the overall prog­
nostic analysis, while SMARCA4-mutant patients who received immu­
notherapy showed a similar prognosis to SMARCA4-unmutated patients. 
Therefore, immunotherapy could be one of the treatment options for 
SMARCA4 mutation-null lung adenocarcinoma. Meanwhile, TMB may 
drive effective anti-tumor immune response and eventually lead to 
sustained clinical response to immunotherapy, while TMB analysis re­
sults showed that SMARCA4-mutant lung adenocarcinoma patients had 
higher TMB (Fig. 3B). These analyses suggested that SMARCA4-mutated 
patients with lung adenocarcinoma may benefit from immunotherapy. 
We then used machine learning to screen genes that influence the 
response to immunotherapy in patients with SMARCA4 mutations. The 
results of ROC curve, confusion matrix and accuracy evaluation showed 
that XGBoost model had better prediction effect than SVM and RF model 
(Fig. 3C-E). Considering the “black box” nature of XGBoost models, we 
introduced SHAP interpretable analysis to visualize the importance 
ranking of features of XGBoost models. The results showed the top five 
genes with influence on the immunotherapy response in SMARCA4 
mutant group (Fig. 3F). Among them, SCGB3A1, CP, HLA-DQB2 and 
SLC5A5 expression were positively correlated with immunotherapy 
response, while RRAD expression was negatively correlated with 
immunotherapy response (Fig. 3G). Prognostic analysis and immuno­
therapy response results of IMvigor210 in the immunotherapy cohort 
showed that the SMARCA4 expression did not significantly affect the 
efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with urothelial carcinoma (Sup­
plementary Fig. 1A-D).
To further explore the mechanism of the difference in immune 
response between SMARCA4 mutant and SMARCA4 low expression 
patients, we analyzed the levels of immune cell infiltration in different 
types of patients. Our results indicated that nearly half of the SMARCA4- 
mutant patients had SMARCA4 expression of <10 % in TCGA-LUAD 
cohort (19/45); accordingly, we defined patients with SMARCA4 
expression <10 % as SMARCA4 low-express group to explore the 
treatment difference between SMARCA4 mutant and SMARCA4 low- 
express patients in TCGA-LUAD cohort. The clinical information of the 
two groups is shown in the Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary 
Table 5, in which the SMARCA4 mutant group had more male patients, 
while there was no significant difference in the SMARCA4 low-express 
group. Immune-infiltration analysis revealed that, when compared 
with the SMARCA4 non-mutant group, the SMARCA4-mutant group had 
a lower infiltration level of resting CD4+ memory T cells, resting mast 
cells and resting dendritic cells, but a higher infiltration level of M0 
macrophages (Fig. 3H). When compared with the SMARCA4-high 
expression group, the SMARCA4 low-expression group only showed a 
slight increase in the infiltration level of γδT cells and neutrophils 
(Supplementary Fig. 1E), which may be the underlying reason for the 
difference in the immunotherapy response between the SMARCA4- 
mutant group and the SMARCA4 low-expression group.
3.5. Response of SMARCA4 mutation group and non-mutation group to 
chemotherapy drugs in lung adenocarcinoma
SMARCA4 mutations are associated with poor chemotherapy re­
sponses in lung adenocarcinoma. We analyzed the relationship between 
SMARCA4 mutations and prognosis in patients treated with chemo­
therapy using two lung adenocarcinoma datasets. Patients with 
SMARCA4 mutations exhibited significantly worse OS in MSK, Cancer 
Cell 2023 dataset (Fig. 4A, p < 0.0001). While SMARCA4 mutations had 
no statistically significant impact on OS (p = 0.06) or PFS (p = 0.08) in 
the TCGA-LUAD dataset, the trend indicated shorter OS and PFS in the 
SMARCA4 mutation group compared to the non-mutation group after 
chemotherapy (Fig. 4B and C). The relationship between SMARCA4 
expression and prognosis was also examined in two chemotherapy- 
treated lung adenocarcinoma datasets. In the OncoSG, Nat Genet 2020 
dataset, low SMARCA4 expression was associated with shorter OS 
(Supplementary Fig. 2A, p = 0.046). However, in the TCGA-LUAD 
dataset, SMARCA4 expression did not significantly affect patient prog­
nosis (Supplementary Fig. 2B and C). The impact of SMARCA4 muta­
tions and low expression on sensitivity to common chemotherapies was 
further investigated. SMARCA4 mutation group showed increased 
sensitivity to rapamycin, but no significant change in sensitivity to 
cisplatin, gemcitabine and other chemotherapy agents (Fig. 4D). The

3.6. SMARCA4 mutation and low SMARCA4 expression have unique 
functional and metabolic features
To investigate the regulatory pathways influenced by SMARCA4 in 
lung adenocarcinoma, KEGG, GO, GSEA and GSVA enrichment analyses 
were conducted on four groups of differentially expressed genes. In vitro 
enrichment analysis of lung adenocarcinoma cell line NCI-H1299 
showed that SMARCA4 was closely related to amino acid metabolism, 
ferroptosis, apoptosis and hypoxia. SMARCA4 was also involved in the 
regulation of MAPK, MTOR and PI3K-Akt tumor-related pathways 
(Fig. 5A). In addition to lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, the enrichment 
analysis of ID8 ovarian tumor cells also showed that SMARCA4 function 
was closely related to amino acid metabolism and hypoxia, and was also 
involved in the regulation of MAPK and PI3K-Akt tumor-related path­
ways (Fig. 5B). Enrichment analysis of the TCGA-LUAD cohort showed 
that SMARCA4 mutations mainly affected tyrosine and steroid hormone 
metabolism and ferroptosis, but had no significant effect on tumor- 
related pathways in vivo (Fig. 5C). Cell progression analysis and GSVA 
analysis showed that SMARCA4 mutation was closely related to cell 
cycle and G2/M checkpoint, and regulated oxidative phosphorylation 
(Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). Meanwhile, by analyzing the enrich­
ment analysis results of high and low 10 % expression of SMARCA4 in 
the TCGA lung adenocarcinoma cohort, we found that the expression of 
SMARCA4 mainly affected histone modification in vivo, and influenced 
multiple tumor-related pathways (Fig. 5D). GSVA enrichment analysis 
showed that SMARCA4 expression was negatively correlated with 
oxidative phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. 4A), and GSEA analysis 
showed that SMARCA4 arrested the cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. 4B). 
The main difference in biological processes affected by SMARCA4 
deletion in vivo and in vitro is the regulation of tumor-related pathways 
such as MAPK and PI3K-Akt pathway. In vivo, SMARCA4 deficiency 
affected metabolism and ferroptosis, while SMARCA4 low expression 
mainly affected histone modification and tumor-related pathways. The 
results of enrichment analysis suggested that SMARA4 mutation and low 
expression of SMARCA4 had different effects on cell functions, and 
SMARCA4 also played different biological functions in vivo and in vitro.
We further analyzed the effects of SMARCA4 mutation and 
SMARCA4 low expression on 84 lung adenocarcinoma metabolic path­
ways in a lung adenocarcinoma cohort of TCGA. The results showed that 
low expression of SMARCA4 mainly affected glyoxylate and dicarbox­
ylate metabolism and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 6A and B), while 
SMARCA4 mutation mainly affected fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 6A and 
D). Only one metabolism-related pathway, fatty acid elongation, is 
affected by both of them (Fig. 6C and E).
3.7. SMARCA4 regulates lung adenocarcinoma cell proliferation, DNA 
replication, and cell cycle
Analysis of cellular progression revealed that SMARCA4 expression 
plays a crucial role in regulating cell growth and the cell cycle (Fig. 7A). 
To validate these findings, we investigated the function of SMARCA4 by 
knocking down its expression in lung adenocarcinoma cells (Fig. 7B). 
Cell proliferation assay demonstrated a significant reduction in the 
proliferation rate of lung adenocarcinoma cells following SMARCA4 
knockdown (Fig. 7C). Similarly, the colony formation ability of 
SMARCA4-deficient cells was significantly reduced (Fig. 7D and E). 
Given that previous enrichment analyses suggested a role for SMARCA4 
in regulating mitosis, flow cytometry was performed to assess its impact 
on the cell cycle. The results indicated that SMARCA4 knockdown led to 
significant arrest of lung adenocarcinoma cells in the G2/M phase 
(Fig. 7F and G). Meanwhile, EdU assays further confirmed that 
SMARCA4 knockdown inhibited DNA replication in lung adenocarci­
noma cells (Fig. 7H and I). Western Blot analysis of cell cycle-related 
proteins revealed decreased levels of CDK2 and CyclinB1, both of 
which are essential for G2/M phase progression, in SMARCA4- 
knockdown PC-9 and NCI-H1975 cell lines (Fig. 7J). Additionally, pro­
tein levels of CDK1 and CyclinA2, key regulators of the S phase, were 
also reduced to varying degrees. These findings suggest that SMARCA4 
exerts complex and multifaceted regulatory functions in the cell cycle of 
lung adenocarcinoma cells.
3.8. SMARCA4 affects oxidative stress and apoptosis in lung 
adenocarcinoma
Previous enrichment analyses indicated that SMARCA4 is closely 
associated with oxidative phosphorylation and plays a regulatory role in 
the MTOR and PI3K-Akt pathways in lung adenocarcinoma. To experi­
mentally validate these findings, we conducted functional assays. ROS 
assays revealed a significant increase in oxidative stress levels in lung 
adenocarcinoma cells following SMARCA4 knockdown (Fig. 8A and B). 
Further detection of the expression levels of oxidative stress-related 
molecules showed that the expression levels of SOD and GSH, which 
inhibit the oxidative level of cells, were decreased in different degrees 
after SMARCA4 knockdown, while the level of oxidative stress product 
MDA was increased (Fig. 8C). Western blot results showed that KEAP1- 
NRF2 pathway was activated in response to oxidative stress induced by 
ROS stimulation after SMARCA4 knockdown (Fig. 8D). Concurrently, 
apoptosis assays demonstrated that SMARCA4 knockdown significantly 
elevated apoptosis levels in these cells (Fig. 8E and F). Western blot 
analysis further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying these 
effects. SMARCA4 knockdown significantly inhibited the activation of 
the Erk pathway. Additionally, phosphorylation levels of 4EBP1 and 
p70S6K, key components of the mTOR pathway, were notably 
decreased, suggesting that SMARCA4 influences oxidative stress and 
apoptosis through its modulation of these pathways (Fig. 8G).

3.10. Exploring the effects of SMARCA4 on lung adenocarcinoma in vivo
Apart from its role in promoting tumor growth in vitro, the present 
study also investigated whether SMARCA4 affects lung adenocarcinoma 
cell growth in vivo. SMARCA4-knockdown PC-9 cells were subcutane­
ously injected into nude mice, and the tumor size was measured regu­
larly (Fig. 10A). The results showed that the tumors in nude mice 
injected with PC-9 with SMARCA4 knockdown were significantly 
smaller than those in the control group (Fig. 10B). In addition, 
SMARCA4 knockdown inhibited tumor growth (Fig. 10C).
The present study also investigated the regulation of SMARCA4 on 
oxidative stress and cell cycle-related molecules in vivo. Consistent with 
the in vitro results, SMARCA4 knockdown inhibited the expression of 
CyclinA2 and CDK2, while NRF2 protein level was significantly 
increased (Fig. 10D and E).
3.11. Expression, prognosis and functional analysis of SMARCA4 in pan- 
cancer
To determine whether SMARCA4 functions similarly across various 
tumor types, pan-cancer enrichment and prognostic analyses were 
conducted. Results showed that SMARCA4 is highly expressed in most 
solid tumors, including lung adenocarcinoma and ovarian cancers 
(Fig. 11A). Prognostic analysis revealed that low SMARCA4 expression 
is associated with poor outcomes in esophageal and endometrial can­
cers, while high SMARCA4 expression is a poor prognostic factor in clear 
cell renal cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma (Supplementary 
Fig. 5A and B). Enrichment analyses indicated that SMARCA4 signifi­
cantly regulates the G2/M checkpoint and mitotic spindle across mul­
tiple tumor types, suggesting that the biological processes involving 
SMARCA4 are conserved in different cancers (Fig. 11B).
4. Discussion
Lung adenocarcinoma with low SMARCA4 expression is multifac­
eted. On the one hand, SMARCA4-dNSCLC is considered to be a highly 
malignant type of lung cancer, which is considered to be highly malig­
nant, drug resistant, and lack of effective treatment. On the other hand, 
our in vitro results suggested that SMARCA4 knockdown resulted in 
decreased proliferation, increased apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in lung 
adenocarcinoma cell lines. By comparing the sequencing results of lung 
adenocarcinoma clinical samples from the TCGA-LUAD dataset 
(SMARCA4 high 10 % and low 10 % expression group) and SMARCA4 
knockdown RNA sequencing results, we speculated that the undiffer­
entiated and high-grade malignant features of SMARCA4-dNSCLC could 
not be attributed to the decreased expression of SMARCA4 single gene. It 
is more likely to be caused by a combination of low SMARCA4 expres­
sion, accompanied by highly invasive mutations, and functional 
compensation pathways and molecules. In conclusion, we suggest that 
SMARCA4 is essential for cell survival and function, and knockdown of 
SMARCA4 leads to defective transcription of essential genes, leading to 
impaired cell survival and proliferation, as well as mTORC1 pathway 
activation. In SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma, the survival of 
SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma is due to the compensatory 
increase of other SWI/SNF complex components function, such as 
SMARCA2, and the increased activity of other mechanisms and path­
ways, such as ATR pathway, which gives SMARCA4-deficient lung 
adenocarcinoma its versatility [32]. SMARCA4 mutations were shown 
to be mutually exclusive with mutations in the most common targeted 
oncogene mutations in NSCLC, such as EGFR, ALK, MET, ROS1, and 
RET, with EGFR mutations showing the strongest mutual exclusion [10].

Although current clinical trials have not yielded uniform results on 
whether SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma patients can benefit 
from chemotherapy and immunotherapy, the study of biomarkers and 
tumor biological characteristics of SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocar­
cinoma patients has found potential treatment methods. Due to the 
compensatory role of SMARCA2 in SMARCA4-deletion lung cancer, the 
SMARCA2 degraders PRT3789 and PRT7732 have been applied in the 
treatment of SMARCA4-mutated tumors. Among them, PRT3789 has 
demonstrated certain anti-tumor activity in patients with NSCLC or 
esophageal cancer (NCT06560645, NCT05639751, NCT06682806). 
Studies have shown that loss of SMARCA4 restricts chromatin accessi­
bility and inhibits transcriptional activation of CCND1 (cyclin D1), 
resulting in CCND1 down-regulation. Both SMARCA4-deficient cell lines 
and clinical cohorts show decreased CCND1 expression and sensitivity to 
CDK4/6 inhibitors [35]. There is a synthetic lethal interaction between 
SMARCA4 deficiency and CDK4/6 inhibition, which may be a useful 
therapeutic strategy for SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma. In 
addition to studies targeting cyclins, recent evidence indicates that 
SMARCA4 mutant cells have increased oxygen consumption due to 
reduced glycolytic capacity and increased anabolic responses, and 
SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma both exhibits enhanced 
oxidative phosphorylation [36]. Therefore, targeting oxidative phos­
phorylation may be a promising strategy for SMARCA4-deficient lung 
adenocarcinoma.

drugs targeting the MTOR pathway, cell cycle, and oxidative phos­

drugs such as cisplatin and paclitaxel. Based on multiple enrichment 
analyses, we believed that there were mainly the following possible drug 
resistance mechanisms. In vivo, the mutation of SMARCA4 had a certain 
impact on the cellular drug resistance pathway Abc-transporters and 
multiple cellular metabolic pathways. The low expression of SMARCA4 
affected multiple tumor growth pathways including MTOR and MAPK, 
and led to stemness changes in lung adenocarcinoma cells. The above- 
mentioned series of changes in tumor biology may be the underlying 
mechanism for the insensitivity of SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocar­
cinoma to conventional chemotherapy drugs, and it is also an important 
research direction for the future.
SMARCA4 plays a multifaceted regulatory role in tumor cells and 
cannot be simply classified as either an oncogene or a tumor suppressor 
gene. As SMARCA4 is essential for key biological processes such as cell 
invasion and DNA repair, SMARCA4 knockdown negatively impacts 
tumor cell growth in vitro and in vivo due to co-mutation and other 
compensatory mechanisms. The impact of SMARCA4 mutation or 
deletion on tumor cells may differ from the results observed in in vitro 
experiments. Due to the complex compensatory relationships in 
SMARCA4-deficient lung adenocarcinoma, it is challenging to directly 
explore these mechanisms in vivo using in vitro models. Given the sig­
nificant differences in prognostic analysis, immunotherapy and 
chemotherapy response predictions, and enrichment analysis results 
between SMARCA4 mutation and low expression samples, we believe 
that these represent two different biological subtypes. We recommend 
that clinical studies simultaneously assess SMARCA4 mutation and ex­
pressions, rather than relying solely on immunohistochemistry to detect 
SMARCA4 protein expression. Most importantly, accurate biomarkers 
must be used to identify populations likely to benefit from different 
treatment modalities.

## Abstract

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) requires sustained protein synthesis; the failing myocardium suppresses anabolic processes. Whether they act on one shared architecture or two is untested, because previous comparisons used differently constructed networks. We rebuilt both networks identically on a common universe (10,213 genes), disease samples only, testing preservation bidirectionally. The translation modules, HF purple (153 genes) and HCC magenta (146 genes), are enriched for canonical translation sets (10.7- and 4.0-fold; FDR < 10⁻⁵⁰ and < 10⁻⁷). The HF module is **strongly preserved** in HCC (Z<sub>summary</sub> = 11.03) and the HCC module **weakly to moderately** in HF (Z<sub>summary</sub> = 7.09); about half of all modules show no preservation in either direction. What differs is direction: ten of 15 a priori translation sets are perturbed oppositely in HCC and heart failure (permutation *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁴; 3 of 5 redundancy clusters, *P* = 0.0031), effect sizes correlating at ρ = −0.550. This holds in every independent HCC cohort (9–10 of 15, ρ = −0.55 to −0.76) but fails with an independent RNA-seq heart-failure arm (at most 3 of 15; ρ = −0.46 to +0.21), so it is a property of transplant-stage myocardium, not HF generally. A same-organ non-malignant control (GSE89377) shows the hepatic side is largely **not cancer-specific**: cirrhosis and HCC move alike on 12 of 15 sets (ρ = +0.668); the program peaks in chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis and falls with malignancy, leaving a residual in three aminoacyl-tRNA and mitochondrial sets. After tissue-composition adjustment, ATF4 and MYC remain the strongest correlates of the canonical translation score, but these bulk-tissue associations do not establish regulation. No translation score was prognostic in any HCC cohort. This separates a shared architecture from a disease-specific direction of association, and the hepatic half of the contrast is shared with non-malignant chronic liver disease.

## Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is an aggressive malignancy whose growth is sustained by unrestrained proliferation, which in turn demands a dramatically expanded capacity for protein synthesis [1,2]. Translation, the final and most energetically expensive step of gene expression, is central to this demand [3]. In HCC, oncogenic pathways converge on the translational apparatus to upregulate ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis [4,5], and translational control is increasingly being recognized as a targetable vulnerability in cancer [6,7]. MYC, a master regulator of both proliferation and translation, is among the most frequently activated oncogenes in HCC [8].

Translation-related transcriptional programs, however, are not uniformly activated across disease states. Heart failure (HF), a chronic degenerative condition of the energy-depleted myocardium, is characterized by suppression of anabolic processes, including ribosome biogenesis and cap-dependent translation, as a response associated with energy conservation [9,10]. mTORC1 signaling, a central anabolic regulator of translation, is inhibited in the failing heart [11,12]. These mirror-image pressures on the same molecular machinery raise a question that has two separable halves: is the *architecture* of the translation-related transcriptional program the same in the two diseases, and, if it is, does the *direction* of its association with disease state differ [13]?

Existing transcriptomic studies on translation-related programs have largely been confined to single-disease contexts, identifying differentially expressed genes, co-expression modules, or prognostic signatures within one disease at a time [13]. Cross-disease comparative transcriptomics provides a direct strategy, but only if the two networks are built comparably: when module discovery uses different gene filters, different soft-thresholding rules, and different detection functions in the two diseases, a difference in module composition between them is uninterpretable, because the analysis cannot distinguish a biological difference from a methodological one. The two halves of the question therefore have different evidential requirements. The architectural half requires formal preservation testing between networks that differ in nothing but the disease. The directional half requires effect sizes estimated under a null that permutes samples rather than gene-set labels, and it requires same-organ disease controls, because a tumour-versus-adjacent-normal contrast cannot by itself separate a malignant program from a program that chronic disease of the same organ has already switched on.

Here we present a fully reproducible cross-disease computational framework that addresses both halves within a single frozen configuration. We (i) construct the HF and HCC co-expression networks under identical settings on a common gene universe, using disease samples only in each disease so that disease state cannot drive either network's structure; (ii) test formally whether the translation modules are preserved between diseases, in both directions; (iii) quantify pathway-level perturbation direction with Hedges *g* and a sample-level permutation null; (iv) test the same-organ controls needed to attribute the hepatic signal to malignancy rather than to chronic liver disease; and (v) examine the transcriptional correlates of the canonical translation score under adjustment for tissue composition.

## Materials and Methods

### Data acquisition and preprocessing

Datasets were selected on the following criteria: (i) availability of both disease and non-disease control samples within the same study, (ii) sample size ≥ 100 where the cohort was used for network construction or primary inference, and (iii) publicly available expression matrices.

**GSE57338 (HF discovery and network-construction cohort).** Left ventricular samples, platform GPL11532 (Affymetrix HuGene 1.1 ST Array). Probes were retained when log₂ intensity ≥ 4 in ≥ 20% of samples and mapped to gene symbols via the hugene11sttranscriptcluster.db annotation package, with multiple probes averaged per gene.

**GSE141198 (HCC discovery and network-construction cohort).** *n* = 148 HCC tumours (RNA-seq). In v1 this cohort was described as a validation set; in the harmonised pipeline it is the HCC network-construction cohort, matched in role to GSE57338 on the HF side.

**TCGA-LIHC (HCC primary inference cohort).** *n* = 424 samples downloaded from the GDC portal (371 primary tumour + 50 solid tissue normal + 3 recurrent tumour), RNA-seq, using `assay(se, "unstranded")`. The three recurrent tumour samples were excluded by the sample-type filter (`sample_type %in% c("Primary Tumor", "Solid Tissue Normal")`), leaving *n* = 421 (371 tumour, 50 normal); 53 patients contribute both a tumour and an adjacent-normal sample. The justification for excluding the recurrent tumours is that three samples cannot form an analysable arm, and that a recurrent lesion has already been exposed to prior treatment and undergone further clonal evolution. DESeq2 [14] variance-stabilising transformation was applied (`blind = TRUE`, `nsub = 1000`, design = ~1), followed by retention of genes with counts ≥ 10 in ≥ 20% of samples. The reason for `blind = TRUE` is that the transformed matrix feeds only unsupervised steps — the co-expression network and the ssGSEA scoring — so no differential-expression test is performed on this matrix anywhere in the manuscript; the effect sizes are computed from the transformed scores by the models described below. Ensembl identifiers were converted with org.Hs.eg.db.

**GSE14520.** Loaded split by platform: GPL3921 (Affymetrix HT Human Genome U133A Array), *n* = 445 (225 tumour, 220 adjacent non-tumour); GPL571 (Affymetrix HG-U133A), *n* = 41 (22 tumour, 19 non-tumour). v1 reported this series as a single *n* = 221 GPL3921 cohort; the platform split is imposed by the data and is reported per platform and per group throughout (S11 Table).

**GSE76427.** *n* = 167 (115 tumour, 52 adjacent non-tumour), GPL10558 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V4.0). v1 reported *n* = 115 tumours only; the control arm is included here.

**GSE116250.** *n* = 64 (50 cardiomyopathy — 37 dilated, 13 ischaemic — and 14 non-failing donor hearts), RNA-seq. Used as an independent HF replication cohort.

**GSE141910.** A 366-sample cardiomyopathy collection: 166 dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), 28 hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), 6 peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), and 166 non-failing donors. In v1 this series was used only through its 28-sample HCM arm, described as an HCM cohort. In v2 all 200 diseased samples are pooled as one "cardiomyopathy versus non-failing" contrast, and the aetiology-specific arms are reported as a sensitivity analysis (S22b–d Tables). The pooled group is therefore 83% DCM, and HCM is a hypertrophic rather than a failing phenotype; this heterogeneity is a limitation of the cohort, not of the contrast, and it is why every stratified arm is reported rather than only the pooled result.

**GSE89377 (same-organ, non-malignant liver disease control).** GPL6947 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V3), *n* = 107 samples spanning the multistep hepatocarcinogenesis spectrum: normal liver (13), chronic hepatitis low grade (8), chronic hepatitis high grade (12), cirrhosis (12), dysplastic nodule low grade (11), dysplastic nodule high grade (11), early HCC (5), and HCC (35, comprising three subgroups pooled). Because every HCC cohort otherwise contrasts tumour against non-tumour tissue from the same organ, this cohort is the only one in the study that can separate a malignant translation program from one that chronic liver disease has already established. Processing: probes were retained when detected (*p* < 0.05) in ≥ 50% of samples (17,793 of 48,803), log₂-transformed, quantile-normalised, and collapsed to gene symbols using the GPL6947 annotation (11,756 genes). Sample identity was verified rather than assumed: the sample-title prefixes (`N-`, `FL-`, `FH-`, `CS-`, `DL-`, `DH-`, `eHCC-`, `TG1-`…) were decoded independently and required to agree with the phenotype characteristic sample by sample; agreement was 107/107, and the pipeline halts on any mismatch.

### Harmonised co-expression network construction

A single frozen configuration file (`R00_config.R`) defines every parameter used below and is `source()`d by every analysis script; the configuration hash is recorded alongside each output table.

**Common gene universe.** Each platform was expression-filtered, mapped to symbols, and collapsed independently; the intersection of the two resulting symbol sets defines the analysis universe `U` (*n* = 10,213 genes). No variance pre-filtering was applied at any stage. Variance-based top-*N* selection, which v1 used, systematically removes ribosomal protein genes — the very genes under study — and would have made the two diseases incomparable. The complete loss chain from platform feature to `U` is reported per platform (S1 Table).

**Samples used.** Both networks were built on **disease samples only** — 177 failing left ventricles (82 DCM, 95 ICM) for HF and 148 HCC tumours (GSE141198) — so that disease state cannot drive the module structure of either network. Sensitivity networks built on all 313 GSE57338 samples and on TCGA-LIHC are reported separately.

**Soft threshold.** β was selected by rule, never by hand. The primary rule is the smallest power whose signed scale-free topology fit reaches R² ≥ 0.85; if the data never reach that cut, the power maximising the fit is used and the fact that the cut was not reached is reported. For HF the cut is reached at β = 14 (signed R² = 0.861). For HCC the cut is never reached; the fit is maximised at β = 5 (R² = 0.790). To hold the two networks to the same stringency, both were built at the **more stringent of the two rule-implied powers**, β = 14, at which the HCC fit is R² = 0.778 (S5 Table). The per-disease-optimal β sensitivity networks are also reported.

**Network construction.** Both diseases used the identical call — `WGCNA::blockwiseModules` [15,16] with `networkType = "signed"`, `TOMType = "signed"`, `minModuleSize = 30`, `deepSplit = 2`, `mergeCutHeight = 0.25`, `pamRespectsDendro = FALSE`, `maxBlockSize = 5000`, `randomSeed = 42` — and the parameter lists are compared element by element at run time, with the script aborting on any mismatch. This replaces v1's asymmetric construction, in which the HF network was built by a manual correlation → adjacency → TOM → `cutreeDynamic` procedure while the HCC network was built with `blockwiseModules`.

### Translation module identification

Modules were not designated by hand or by keyword matching. The a priori translation gene sets (below) were scored by ssGSEA in each network's own expression matrix, and each module's score was its mean ssGSEA score across its member genes. The module with the highest score was designated the translation module. Over-representation of the a priori sets within each module was then computed as a one-sided hypergeometric test against `U`, with BH correction across modules [17] (S9 Table). The full module × set score matrix is published (S8 Table) so that the designation can be recomputed from the deposited data.

### Module preservation

Preservation was tested with `WGCNA::modulePreservation` [18] in both directions (HF modules tested in the HCC network and vice versa), 200 permutations, on the common universe `U`, with composite *Z*<sub>summary</sub> and median rank statistics. Modules were classified by the conventional *Z*<sub>summary</sub> bands (> 10 strong, 2–10 weak to moderate, < 2 no evidence). `modulePreservation` subsamples modules larger than 1,000 genes (`maxModuleSize = 1000`), so the three largest modules in each direction are represented by a random 1,000-gene subsample (S19 Table legend).

### Module-internal robustness

Intra-modular topological overlap was summarised as the mean off-diagonal signed TOM among a module's genes, computed with `WGCNA::TOMsimilarity`. **Null specification.** TOM is connectivity-normalised — its denominator is min(*k*<sub>i</sub>, *k*<sub>j</sub>) − *a*<sub>ij</sub> — so a uniformly random gene set is not an exchangeable null unless the module's genes share the universe's connectivity distribution, which they do not (HF purple mean *k* = 6.6 versus 15.2 universe-wide; HCC magenta 21.1 versus 74.1). Under a uniform null the HCC module therefore returns no enrichment even though a connectivity-free measure shows it to be the tighter of the two modules. The primary null is accordingly **connectivity-matched**: 20 quantile bins of *k* over `U`, with each module gene replaced by a uniformly drawn non-module member of its own bin. Both the unmatched (uniform) and matched nulls are reported (S32 Table) for transparency. Because bin-matching collapses the null's variance, *Z* values are not comparable between the two nulls, and the interpretable quantity is the observed-to-null mean ratio. Stability was assessed by 90%-retention jackknife resampling, reporting the coefficient of variation of the mean intra-modular connectivity (the quantity comparable to v1's jackknife figure) separately from the median per-gene CV. Cross-disease coherence — the mean pairwise correlation of a module's genes measured in the other disease's expression matrix — was tested against a uniform null, which is valid here because the mean pairwise correlation is not connectivity-normalised.

### Pathway scores and effect sizes

Gene set sources were defined a priori by explicit membership rather than by keyword matching. **Tier A** (15 sets) comprises the core cytosolic and mitochondrial translation machinery: KEGG ribosome and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, and the Reactome translation, initiation, elongation, tRNA aminoacylation (cytosolic and mitochondrial), rRNA processing (cytosolic and mitochondrial), SRP-dependent co-translational targeting, ribosome-associated quality control, and mitochondrial translation sets. **Tier B** (21 sets) adds tRNA and rRNA processing and modification sets. **Tier C** (33 sets) is the broad transcription-level set used in v1, dominated by viral and miRNA-mediated translation sets; it is retained only for continuity with v1 and its members are labelled as such. MYC targets, mTORC1, UPR, and all viral and miRNA sets are excluded from Tiers A and B. Set redundancy was quantified by Jaccard hierarchical clustering at cut height 0.7, and the effective number of independent tests was computed by both the Nyholt [19] and Li & Ji [20] methods (S15, S16 Tables).

ssGSEA scores were computed with GSVA [21,22] (`ssgseaParam(minSize = 5, maxSize = 500)`, `normalize = TRUE`) using an identical parameter object in every cohort. Disease-associated effect size was Hedges *g* [23], computed as *g* = *J* · (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/*s*<sub>p</sub>, where the pooled standard deviation is *n*-weighted, *s*<sub>p</sub> = sqrt( ((*n*₁ − 1)*s*₁² + (*n*₂ − 1)*s*₂²) / (*n*₁ + *n*₂ − 2) ), and the small-sample correction is *J* = 1 − 3/(4*df* − 1) with *df* = *n*₁ + *n*₂ − 2 (equivalently, *J* = 1 − 3/(4(*n*₁ + *n*₂) − 9)); unequal group sizes and unequal variances are handled by the weighting in *s*<sub>p</sub> and by *J* respectively. Every estimate is reported with a 2,000-resample bootstrap confidence interval. A pathway was called a **mirror** only when it met all three conditions in both diseases: opposite signs, |*g*| ≥ 0.20, and a bootstrap CI excluding zero. Significance of the mirror count was assessed by **sample-level permutation** — disease labels were permuted within each cohort and the entire pipeline recomputed, 10,000 iterations, seed 42 — and evaluated both at the level of individual sets and at the level of the Jaccard redundancy clusters, so that correlated sets cannot inflate the count.

### Cohort heterogeneity and same-organ controls

Between-cohort heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's *Q* and *I*² [24] within each disease. Aetiology was tested directly rather than offered as an explanation: the DCM and ICM arms of GSE57338 were analysed separately, the DCM/HCM/PPCM arms of GSE141910 separately, and the DCM arms of the two cohorts were compared with each other (S22b–d Tables). Same-technology pairings were constructed to the extent the available cohorts permit, and the resulting confound is stated explicitly rather than assumed away.

### Clinical covariate adjustment

Patient age and sex were available in all three heart-failure cohorts (GSE57338, GSE116250, GSE141910) and in TCGA-LIHC, and in those four cohorts every disease-versus-control effect was refitted with both in the model, `score ~ group + age + sex` (S37 Table). The group effect is reported on the same fully standardised scale used for the composition-adjusted models, with a bootstrap confidence interval built by resampling within each group exactly as in the primary analysis, so that the mirror gate (opposite signs, |*g*| ≥ 0.20, CI excluding zero in both diseases) applies to the adjusted effects without modification. Adjusted and unadjusted fits use the same complete-case sample set, so the two are paired and any change is attributable to the covariates rather than to the samples; the unadjusted standardised effect reproduces the primary Hedges *g* at ρ = 1.000 in all four cohorts, which is what licenses the comparison. The four cohorts that are not adjusted here are absent for reasons that are properties of the deposited annotation rather than choices: GSE141198 has no control arm, the GSE14520 series matrices carry no age or sex column, and in GSE76427 age and sex are recorded for the 115 tumour samples and for none of the 52 adjacent-normal samples, so they cannot enter a two-arm model. NYHA class and left-ventricular ejection fraction appear in none of the three heart-failure series matrices; no adjustment for disease severity is claimed.

### Tissue composition adjustment

Marker-based composition scores were computed by ssGSEA for hepatocyte, cardiomyocyte, fibroblast, proliferation, and a pan-immune panel, and used as covariates in partial correlations relating transcription-factor expression to the canonical translation score. **The hepatocyte score is the tumour-purity covariate in the liver cohorts.** Resected HCC tissue is diluted to varying degrees by non-malignant hepatocytes and stroma, so a higher hepatocyte score indicates a larger non-malignant fraction, and adjusting for it is what is meant by "adjusting for tumour purity" wherever that adjustment is reported below; the caveat is that malignant hepatocytes share these marker genes, so the score indexes the non-malignant fraction of the sample rather than a purity estimate proper, and it is used as a proxy for that reason. In the myocardial cohorts the cardiomyocyte score plays the corresponding role. Marker coverage differs sharply by platform: the GSE57338 array retains only 10 of 24 immune panel markers, losing the entire lymphoid panel (CD3D/E/G, CD2, CD8A, CD19, MS4A1, GNLY, KLRD1, FCGR3A, CXCL9, CXCL10, FOXP3, CTLA4) as well as the lymphocyte and proliferation panels; its "immune-adjusted" model is therefore myeloid-only, and results from that cohort are labelled accordingly (S23–S26 Tables).

### Translation scores and clinical association

Two score families were defined identically in every cohort. The **canonical translation score (TCS)** is the mean of z-scored ssGSEA values over the 15 Tier A sets; it is disease-agnostic by construction, so it cannot be the source of a cross-disease difference. The **module eigengene scores** project each disease's translation module into a cohort as the kME-weighted mean z-expression of its genes, using the weights from the originating network, and are reported in both directions. v1's TATS (the mean z-score of its 227-gene green module) is superseded; the number of contributing genes differs by cohort and is reported per cohort (S27 Table). Overall survival was analysed by Kaplan–Meier log-rank and Cox proportional hazards with TCS as a continuous per-IQR variable and as a median split, in each HCC cohort with usable outcome annotation.

**What these scores measure, and what they do not.** Both score families are derived from transcript abundance. A higher score means that the transcripts of a translation-associated gene set are coordinately more abundant in one group than in the other; it is not a measurement of ribosome biogenesis, of translational regulation, of translational efficiency, or of protein synthesis, and no claim about those quantities is made anywhere in this manuscript. The wording follows from that limit. Findings built on these scores are described as transcriptional signatures — statements about which transcripts co-vary, not about translation — and the word *activity* is not used of them, because a coordinated change in ribosomal-protein transcript levels is consistent with a change in the rate of protein synthesis without establishing one.

### Transcription factor association

Candidate transcription factors were the MYC family (MYC, MYCN, MYCL), the E2F family, mTOR pathway components (MTOR, RPTOR, TSC1, TSC2), translation initiation factors (EIF4E, EIF4G1, EIF4EBP1), and stress-responsive factors (ATF4, ATF6, DDIT3, XBP1, ERN1, EIF2AK3, HIF1A, NFE2L2, TP53). Spearman correlations with TCS were computed and then recomputed as partial correlations adjusting for tissue composition, with BH FDR correction within each cohort and score, and reported for every cohort rather than one. Members of the translation gene sets are flagged so that a correlation driven by shared membership can be identified. These are associations in bulk tissue; no causal or upstream-regulatory claim is made.

## Results

### Harmonised cross-disease networks identify one translation module in each disease

To make a cross-disease comparison possible at all, the two co-expression networks were rebuilt under a single frozen configuration. Both used the same gene universe (*U* = 10,213 genes, the intersection of the two platforms after independent filtering, with no variance pre-filtering), the same detection function and parameters, and the same soft-thresholding rule; and each was built on **disease samples only** — 177 failing left ventricles for HF and 148 HCC tumours for GSE141198 — so that disease state could not drive either network's module structure (Fig 1a, b; S4, S5 Tables). The shared power, β = 14, is the more stringent of the two rule-implied values (HF reaches the R² ≥ 0.85 criterion at β = 14; the HCC fit never reaches it and is maximised at β = 5, but for comparability both were built at β = 14, where the HCC fit is R² = 0.778). This resolves v1's β = 17/β = 4 asymmetry, which arose from per-dataset optimisation rather than from a rule.

The harmonised networks contain 23 named co-expression modules in HF and 20 in HCC (excluding the unassigned grey bin; S4 Table). Applying the a priori translation gene sets to each module without any manual designation identified **HF purple (153 genes)** and **HCC magenta (146 genes)** as the translation modules (Fig 1c; S8, S9 Tables). HF purple contains 73 Tier A translation genes against 6.8 expected — a 10.7-fold enrichment (hypergeometric FDR = 5.7 × 10⁻⁵⁷) — and HCC magenta contains 26 against 6.5 expected, a 4.0-fold enrichment (FDR = 1.2 × 10⁻⁸). In both networks the translation machinery genes are concentrated in this one module rather than spread across the network, which is the result the harmonised pipeline produces and is not what v1's differentially-filtered networks produced.

### Translation-module architecture is preserved between diseases, asymmetrically

v1 inferred disease-specific network organisation from the limited gene-level overlap between its two modules, using Fisher's exact test on gene counts. That inference is not supported by a formal test, and we replaced it with one. `modulePreservation` was run in both directions on the common universe with 200 permutations (Fig 1d; S19, S20 Tables).

The HF translation module is **strongly preserved** in the HCC network (*Z*<sub>summary</sub> = 11.03, above the conventional strong-preservation threshold of 10; median rank 3; rank 2 of 23 named modules; BH FDR = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴¹). The HCC translation module is **weakly to moderately preserved** in the HF network (*Z*<sub>summary</sub> = 7.09, median rank 5, rank 6 of 20; FDR = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁵). The asymmetry is real and is reported rather than averaged: the HF translation module transfers to HCC better than the reverse. It is also not universal — across all named modules, 3 of 23 are strongly preserved and 11 of 23 show no evidence of preservation in the HF → HCC direction, and 2 of 20 and 6 of 20 respectively in the HCC → HF direction (Fig 1d, e). Roughly half of the transcriptome's modular structure is not shared between these diseases; the translation modules are among the minority that is.

The consequence for the rest of the manuscript is an ordering constraint. Because the translation-module **architecture is shared**, the two diseases cannot be contrasted on the basis of module composition, and v1's "disease-specific network remodelling" framing is withdrawn. What remains to be tested is whether the *direction* of the modules' association with disease state differs.

### The modules are internally robust under a connectivity-matched null

Before interpreting any between-disease difference, we verified that the designated modules are genuine co-expression structures rather than consequences of the soft-threshold. A uniform random gene-set null is not exchangeable for TOM, which is normalised by connectivity (min(*k*<sub>i</sub>, *k*<sub>j</sub>) − *a*<sub>ij</sub>), and both designated modules sit well below the universe's connectivity (HF purple mean *k* = 6.6 versus 15.2; HCC magenta 21.1 versus 74.1). Under the uniform null the HCC module returns *Z* = −0.14 (*P* = 0.54) — apparently not robust — whereas a connectivity-free measure of the same property gives mean pairwise *r* = 0.3916 against a null of 0.0239 (*Z* = 26.0), the opposite conclusion (S36 Table). The primary null is therefore **connectivity-matched** (20 quantile bins of *k*; S32 Table).

Under the matched null both modules are strongly cohesive: HF purple mean intra-modular TOM = 0.0079 against 0.0027 unmatched (*Z* = 13.77; observed/null ratio 2.87) and 11.01-fold over the connectivity-matched null; HCC magenta 0.0180 against 0.0183 unmatched (ratio 0.98) and 4.68-fold over the matched null. The 0.98 → 4.68 change is the whole effect of correcting the null, and it is why the ratio rather than the *Z* is reported: bin-matching collapses the null's variance and inflates *Z* mechanically, which for the same two modules takes *Z* to 449.7 (HF) and 87.8 (HCC), so *Z* values across the two nulls are not comparable. Jackknife resampling at 90% retention gives a coefficient of variation of the mean intra-modular connectivity of 2.66% (HF) and 2.48% (HCC) — the quantity comparable to v1's reported 1.6% — while the median per-gene CV is ~50% in both, which at β = 14 reflects the adjacency being dominated by each gene's few strongest partners rather than instability.

Cross-disease coherence is asymmetric in the same direction as the preservation result: the HF module's genes have mean pairwise *r* = 0.3256 when measured in the HCC matrix (*Z* = 22.2 against a uniform null, which is valid here because mean pairwise correlation is not connectivity-normalised), whereas the HCC module's genes have *r* = 0.14 in the HF matrix (*Z* = 13.5). Both are far above chance; neither is symmetric.

### Opposite-direction pathway perturbation of translation-related programs

With shared architecture established, we tested the direction of the association. ssGSEA scores were computed for the a priori gene sets in TCGA-LIHC (371 tumours versus 50 adjacent normals) and GSE57338 (177 failing versus 136 non-failing), and Hedges *g* was computed for each set in each disease (Fig 2a; S14 Table). Across all 41 scored sets the cross-disease correlation of effect sizes is ρ = −0.455; across the 15 Tier A translation sets it is **ρ = −0.550**.

Applying the mirror criterion — opposite signs, |*g*| ≥ 0.20 in both diseases, and a bootstrap CI excluding zero in both — **10 of 15 Tier A sets** are mirror-perturbed, against a sample-level permutation null mean of 0.11 sets (permutation *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁴). Because the Tier A sets are not independent, the test was repeated at the level of Jaccard redundancy clusters: 3 of 5 clusters are mirror clusters against a null mean of 0.06 (*P* = 0.0031). For continuity with v1, the same analysis on Tier C gives 21 of 33 sets (*P* = 1 × 10⁻⁴; 14 of 19 clusters, *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁴). The five Tier A sets that do **not** mirror are KEGG aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and all four mitochondrial translation sets (mitochondrial translation, mitochondrial translation elongation, mitochondrial tRNA aminoacylation, and mitochondrial rRNA processing). The mirror is therefore a property of the cytosolic translation machinery — ribosome, initiation, elongation, tRNA aminoacylation, rRNA processing, SRP-dependent targeting, and ribosome quality control — and not of translation in general.

We note that v1 stated that "all 33 translation-related pathways showed positive effect sizes in HCC". That statement is false: the v1 submission's own S2 Table contains six negative values among those pathways, and the sentence has been removed. v1 also reported a binomial test of *P* < 0.0001 for its mirror count; no implementation of that test exists in the analysis code, and for 24/33 at *p* = 0.5 the exact two-sided binomial value is *P* = 0.0135. It is replaced here by the sample-level permutation test described above, which is both correct and more stringent, and against which the observed count still clears by a wide margin.

### The mirror is a property of the failing-myocardium cohort, not of heart failure generally

The mirror is reproduced against **every** independent HCC cohort tested: with GSE57338 as the HF arm, Tier A effect sizes correlate at ρ = −0.550 (TCGA-LIHC, 10/15 sets mirroring), −0.702 (GSE14520 GPL3921, 9/14), −0.764 (GSE14520 GPL571, 9/15), and −0.587 (GSE76427, 9/14) (Fig 2d; S18 Table). The hepatic side of the contrast is therefore reproducible across platforms and patient populations.

The cardiac side is not. Across all 12 cohort pairings, no cell with an independent HF RNA-seq cohort exceeds 3 of 15 mirror sets, and the effect-size correlations run from −0.393 to +0.211 against GSE116250 and from −0.457 to −0.059 against GSE141910, against 9–10 of 15 and ρ = −0.59 to −0.76 in every cell with GSE57338 (Fig 2d). A technology-level permutation test, which pools the HCC array cohorts against each HF cohort and is reported as such because it is a different grouping from the cohort-by-cohort matrix, agrees: 2 of 14 Tier A sets against GSE116250 (*P* = 0.174) and 0 of 14 against GSE141910 (*P* = 1.00). One Tier C row does not follow this pattern — HCC array versus GSE141910 at Tier C gives 11 of 27 mirror sets (*P* = 2 × 10⁻⁴) while its Tier A row is null — and we report that inconsistency rather than smoothing it over; it is most plausibly driven by the low-coverage cohorts within Tier C, but we cannot exclude a Tier-C-specific effect.

Three candidate explanations for the non-replication were tested rather than asserted. **Heterogeneity.** Between-cohort *Q* and *I*² were computed per set within each disease: across the 15 Tier A sets the median *I*² = 88.2% for HF and 89.7% for HCC, with median *p*<sub>Q</sub> = 2.1 × 10⁻⁴ and 2.2 × 10⁻⁶ respectively, and 83% of sets showing significant heterogeneity within each disease (S21 Table). The discordance is therefore not a property of a few outlier sets. **Aetiology** was tested and excluded as the explanation: the DCM and ICM arms of GSE57338 agree with each other (both 11 of 13 gated sets mirror; mean *g* = −0.291 and −0.431), so the discovery result does not depend on which HF aetiology was sampled; and GSE141910's DCM arm alone — 166 versus 166, the aetiology that dominates that collection, and a precisely balanced design — still gives 1 of 13 mirror sets. Same-aetiology, same-organ cohorts disagree: DCM in GSE57338 versus DCM in GSE141910 gives ρ = +0.350 with only 4 of 15 Tier A sets pointing the same way. **Platform** is not a clean explanation either, because the primary comparison is itself cross-technology (array HF versus RNA-seq HCC) and is among the strongest rows in the table. **Clinical covariates** are excluded as well, in the sense that they cannot account for the pattern: age and sex are published for all three HF cohorts and for TCGA-LIHC, and refitting every disease-versus-control effect with both in the model (`score ~ group + age + sex`; S37 Table) leaves the mirror count unchanged — GSE57338 gives 10 of 15 Tier A sets before and after adjustment, GSE141910 1 of 15 both times, GSE116250 1 of 15 both times — with the discovery cohort's effect-size correlation moving only from ρ = −0.550 to −0.461 (S38 Table). Median attenuation of |*g*| is 2.9% and no fit is collinear (maximum VIF 1.03 across 163 set–cohort fits). Two limitations are recorded rather than passed over: sex is itself associated with several translation sets (33 of 60 Tier A set–cohort fits at *P* < 0.05, against 16 of 60 for age), so these scores are not sex-invariant; and NYHA class and left-ventricular ejection fraction are absent from the series matrices of all three HF cohorts, so disease severity cannot be adjusted for from the deposited data. What the data support is narrower and more useful than v1's "magnitude varies with cohort characteristics": the mirror belongs to transplant-stage failing myocardium as sampled by GSE57338, and is not recovered in either independent HF RNA-seq cohort. The abstract, title, and conclusions are scoped accordingly.

### The hepatic arm is shared with non-malignant chronic liver disease

Every HCC cohort in the analysis contrasts tumour against non-tumour tissue from the same organ, so on its own the hepatic arm of the mirror is equally consistent with a liver-disease effect — inflammation, fibrosis, regeneration — and with a cancer-specific one. GSE89377, a multistep hepatocarcinogenesis series with an explicit non-malignant arm, separates the two, because cirrhosis is diseased liver that is not malignant (Fig 3).

The cohort's HCC arm was validated before it was interpreted: against the four HCC cohorts already in the pipeline, sign agreement on Tier A sets was 100% (TCGA-LIHC; ρ = 0.607), 100% (GSE14520 GPL3921; ρ = 0.790), 93% (GSE14520 GPL571; ρ = 0.596), and 100% (GSE76427; ρ = 0.833). It reproduces the established HCC pattern, and its HCC arm also mirrors GSE57338 (ρ = −0.679).

Within GSE89377, where cohort, platform, and pipeline are held constant, cirrhosis and HCC move in the same direction on **12 of 15 Tier A sets** — 11 up in both, 1 down in both (80% concordance; ρ = +0.668; S35 Table). Across all 40 scored sets the concordance is 60% (21 up, 3 down; ρ = +0.532). The bulk cytosolic translation program that forms the mirror is therefore **already engaged in non-malignant chronic liver disease**. The three discordant Tier A sets are precisely the aminoacyl-tRNA and mitochondrial family: Reactome tRNA aminoacylation (*g* = −0.60 in cirrhosis versus +0.47 in HCC), Reactome mitochondrial tRNA aminoacylation (−0.85 versus +0.35), and KEGG aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (−0.75 versus +0.22). The cancer-associated residual is concentrated there.

The trajectory across the ordered spectrum is non-monotone, which rules out a dose–response reading in either direction. For KEGG ribosome, Tier A effect sizes run 0.61 (chronic hepatitis, low grade) → 1.40 (chronic hepatitis, high grade) → 2.03 (cirrhosis) → 1.07 (dysplastic nodule, low grade) → 0.15 (dysplastic nodule, high grade) → 0.20 (early HCC) → 0.47 (HCC). The hepatic translation program is highest in non-malignant chronic liver disease and falls as malignancy emerges; it is not switched on at the malignant transition.

A magnitude comparison — cirrhosis exceeds HCC in |*g*| on 14 of 15 Tier A sets within GSE89377 (mean |*g*| 1.13 versus 0.51) — invited the objection that this cohort's HCC arm is simply compressed. It is: mean |*g*| across the HCC cohorts is 1.44 (GSE14520 GPL3921), 1.10 (GSE76427), 1.08 (GSE14520 GPL571), 0.74 (TCGA-LIHC), and 0.54 (GSE89377). The deficit is nevertheless cohort-wide rather than specific to the translation sets — the GSE89377/TCGA-LIHC |*g*| ratio is 0.68 for Tier A sets versus 0.76 for non-Tier-A sets — so the internal cirrhosis-versus-HCC comparison is interpretable, both arms carrying the same compression. Because compression preserves signs and attenuates magnitudes, the **direction** result is the headline and the magnitude result is not; the magnitude statement is reported only with this check attached.

The consequence for the manuscript is a narrowing. The hepatic arm of the mirror may not be described as cancer-specific reprogramming. It is a translation-associated program shared with non-malignant chronic liver disease, with a smaller cancer-associated component concentrated in the aminoacyl-tRNA and mitochondrial sets. This cohort does not speak to the cardiac arm at all.

### The canonical translation score has no prognostic association in HCC

Because a program can be biologically coherent without being clinically predictive, and because v1 reported a null survival result that should not be quietly dropped, we tested whether the canonical translation score (TCS, the mean z-scored ssGSEA over the 15 Tier A sets) was associated with overall survival. TCS was used in preference to a module-derived score because it is defined identically in every cohort and cannot be the source of a cohort difference; module eigengene scores are reported alongside it in both directions (S28, S29 Tables).

TCS was not associated with overall survival in any cohort with usable annotation, whether modelled as a continuous per-IQR variable or as a median split: TCGA-LIHC (*n* = 421) and GSE141198 (*n* = 148), and no association emerged in the aetiology-stratified analyses (S29 Table). The number of contributing genes differs by cohort — 15 of 15 Tier A sets in most cohorts, 14 in the two low-coverage ones — and is reported per cohort (S27 Table) rather than as a single figure, which is the discrepancy that made v1's "227 genes" unverifiable across cohorts. The absence of a prognostic association indicates that aggregate translation-set transcript abundance reflects a biological state rather than a determinant of outcome.

### Transcriptional correlates of the canonical translation score

Transcription-factor expression was related to TCS by Spearman correlation and then by partial correlation adjusting for tissue composition — in the HCC cohorts that adjustment is for tumour purity (the hepatocyte score defined in the Methods), proliferation and immune content; in HF it is for cardiomyocyte, fibroblast and immune content — with BH FDR correction, in every cohort (S30 Table). In TCGA-LIHC the strongest associations after adjustment are ATF4 (*r*<sub>partial</sub> = +0.237, FDR = 1.6 × 10⁻⁶), MYC (+0.233, FDR = 2.2 × 10⁻⁶), DDIT3 (+0.193, FDR = 8.2 × 10⁻⁵), and EIF4EBP1 (+0.163, FDR = 9.3 × 10⁻⁴), alongside negative associations with EIF2AK3 (−0.452), ERN1 (−0.415), and MTOR (−0.388). The patterns are not stable across cohorts in sign — MYC's association is positive in TCGA-LIHC and in GSE141198 but negative in the translation-score projection onto the HCC module — which is itself informative and is why all cohorts are reported rather than one.

Two features of this analysis must be stated plainly. First, several of the correlated factors are themselves members of translation-related gene sets, and each is flagged as such in the table, so a correlation arising from shared gene membership can be identified rather than assumed away. Second, these are correlations in bulk tissue between transcript abundances. They identify which transcriptional programs co-vary with the canonical translation score; they do not identify upstream regulators, and we make no regulatory claim. v1's description of ATF4 and MYC as "complementary regulatory dimensions" is withdrawn, as is the integrated-stress-response interpretation of the ATF4 association.

## Discussion

This study asked two separable questions about translation-related transcriptional programs in HCC and heart failure: whether the two diseases share one module architecture, and whether the direction of the program's association with disease state differs. The answer to the first is yes, with a caveat about asymmetry; the answer to the second is that the direction does differ in the failing-myocardium cohort, but that the hepatic half of that difference is not specific to malignancy.

The architectural question had not previously been answered, because it had not been asked in a form that could be. v1 compared networks built with different gene filters, different soft-thresholds chosen by per-dataset optimisation, different detection functions, and different module-selection procedures, and inferred disease-specific organisation from limited gene-level overlap; no formal preservation test was performed. Under a harmonised configuration on a common universe, with both networks built on disease samples only and with the identical function and parameters, the formal test gives the opposite answer for the translation modules: the HF module is strongly preserved in HCC (*Z*<sub>summary</sub> = 11.03, rank 2 of 23) and the HCC module is weakly to moderately preserved in HF (7.09, rank 6 of 20). The two diseases share the architecture of this program.

That result also sets the boundary of the claim. The preservation is asymmetric, and it is not general: roughly half of all named modules show no evidence of preservation in either direction, so this is not a statement that the two transcriptomes are organised alike. It is a statement about one program, in a minority of modules, tested both ways.

Against that shared background, the direction of the association differs, and the mirror survives every correction applied to it: 10 of 15 Tier A sets, permutation *P* = 1 × 10⁻⁴, and 3 of 5 redundancy clusters, *P* = 0.0031, with |*g*| ≥ 0.20 and an excluding bootstrap CI required in both diseases. It reproduces against every independent HCC cohort tested (ρ = −0.55 to −0.76 across four cohorts and three platforms). Its scope is nevertheless narrower than v1 implied. The mirror is not recovered when the cardiac arm is an independent HF RNA-seq cohort, and the failure is not explained by aetiology — the DCM and ICM arms of the discovery cohort agree with each other, while the same aetiology sampled in two different cohorts does not (ρ = +0.350; 4/15 sets concordant) — nor by platform, since the primary comparison is itself cross-technology. It is a property of the transplant-stage failing myocardium as sampled here. The tests above replace speculation with a measurement, and the measurement does not support a general HF claim.

The hepatic arm carries the most consequential result. Holding platform and pipeline constant within GSE89377, cirrhosis and HCC move the same way on 12 of 15 Tier A sets (ρ = +0.668), and the program's trajectory across the hepatocarcinogenesis spectrum is non-monotone, peaking in chronic hepatitis and cirrhosis and declining across dysplasia and early HCC. The bulk cytosolic translation program is therefore not a malignant program at all in this analysis — it is a chronic-liver-disease program that HCC happens to sit inside. What remains cancer-associated is a narrower residual: the aminoacyl-tRNA and mitochondrial translation sets, which are the three Tier A sets that discord between cirrhosis and HCC and are also the five that fail to mirror in the primary comparison.
Two caveats apply to the shared hepatic signal. First, no composition correction was applied in the GSE89377 analysis, unlike the main cohorts. Chronically diseased liver and tumour both carry shifted immune, fibrotic, and stromal content relative to normal liver, so part of the signal that cirrhosis and HCC share may be a shared composition shift rather than a shared cell-intrinsic program. Second, the cohort's early-HCC arm contains only five samples, its HCC arm pools three subgroups, and its array pipeline retains 36% of probes and defines an 11,756-gene universe rather than the discovery universe *U*.

ATF4 and MYC remain the strongest correlates of the canonical translation score after adjusting for tissue composition in TCGA-LIHC, but the signs are not stable across cohorts, several correlated factors are themselves members of translation gene sets, and the adjustments available are unequal — the GSE57338 array retains only 10 of 24 immune markers, so its "immune-adjusted" model is myeloid-only. We therefore report these as associations in bulk tissue, ordered across cohorts, with set membership flagged, and draw no regulatory inference. In particular, the association between ATF4 and the canonical translation score is not evidence that the integrated stress response drives translation-related transcription in HCC, and we do not interpret it that way.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The analysis is entirely of bulk transcriptomes, so the programs described are the transcriptional organisation of translation-associated genes, not measurements of translation itself; ribosome profiling would be required to test translational output directly [25]. Co-expression structure in bulk tissue reflects both cell-intrinsic regulation and cellular composition, and although composition adjustment was applied where markers were available, it attenuates rather than eliminates this confound (55 of 103 Tier A set–cohort pairs retain their direction after adjustment, with a median attenuation of 34.1%; S25 Table). The mirror is established for one HF cohort and not the other two, which limits its generality. The HCC cohorts are predominantly of Asian ancestry. `modulePreservation` subsamples modules larger than 1,000 genes, which affects the three largest modules in each direction but neither designated translation module. And the framework remains observational throughout: it can establish that an association is shared, specific, or absent, and it cannot establish causation.

Translation-related transcriptional programs in HCC and heart failure are organised through a **shared module architecture** whose association with disease state points in **opposite directions** in the two diseases. The architectural half is now supported by a formal preservation test rather than inferred from gene-overlap counts, and the directional half is supported by a sample-level permutation null rather than a label permutation. What the cross-disease comparison adds is a boundary that single-disease analyses cannot draw: the hepatic half of the contrast is largely a property of chronic liver disease rather than of malignancy, with a smaller cancer-associated component in the aminoacyl-tRNA and mitochondrial sets, while the cardiac half is recovered in transplant-stage failing myocardium and not in independent heart-failure cohorts of other compositions. Distinguishing a conserved program from a conserved architecture, and a disease-specific signal from a disease-state signal, requires this kind of matched construction. Cross-system integrative frameworks that align molecular programs across organs and diseases are now being assembled for this purpose [26]; the present analysis illustrates both what such a framework can establish and how far its conclusions remain bounded by the composition of the cohorts it draws on.
